1Synergy

Definition (slide)

Synergy: the whole is greater than the sum of its parts.

The word comes from science: an action of two or more substances that gives an effect different from simply adding them up.

In simple words

When people work together properly, the result is more than what they would have made working alone and then adding up. That “extra” is synergy. The slide shows a big glowing 3: the idea is 1 + 1 = 3.

Example

You are great at coding, your friend is great at design. Alone, you each make half a hackathon project. Together, you build a polished app that wins. The win came from combining skills, not just adding them.

Exam line

Synergy means the combined output of a group is greater than the sum of individual outputs (1 + 1 > 2).

2Group vs Team

Every team is a group, but not every group is a team. The difference is synergy.

Work Group

Members interact mainly to share information and make decisions, so that each person can do his or her own part well.

Output = sum of individual work.

Work Team

Members’ individual efforts produce a performance that is greater than the sum of individual inputs.

Output > sum (positive synergy).

PointGroupTeam
GoalShare informationCollective performance
SynergyNeutral (sometimes negative)Positive
AccountabilityIndividualIndividual + mutual
SkillsRandom, variedComplementary (fit together)

The table is the standard textbook (Robbins) comparison, added to help you write a fuller answer. The slide gives the two definitions.

Example

Group: 5 students in a library, each studying their own subject, sometimes sharing notes.
Team: a cricket team. The bowler, keeper and fielders depend on each other; one win belongs to all.

Remember it like this

Group = “we sit together.”   Team = “we win together.”

3The big picture: model of group formation & development

The slide puts the whole chapter in one line. Read it left to right like a story: what kind of group, why it forms, how it grows, what it looks like inside, and what it produces.

Remember it like this

Think of a new hostel wing: What type of group is it? Why did we stick together? How did we go from strangers to family (stages)? What unwritten rules and “seniors” exist (characteristics)? Did we end up happy and productive (results)?

Type → Why → Stages → Inside → Result

4Types of groups

Formal groups are created by the organization (on the org chart, with assigned work). Informal groups form naturally because people want company or share a concern.

👔

Command group

People who report directly to the same manager.

All engineers under one team lead.

🛠️

Task group

People working together to complete a job or task. Can cross departments.

A committee set up to organise the college fest.

🎯

Interest group

People working together to achieve a specific objective that each of them cares about.

Employees uniting to demand a better canteen or flexible hours.

🤝

Friendship group

People brought together because they share common characteristics.

Same hometown, same age, all love cricket, the “lunch gang”.

The model also lists Team as a third formal group.

Remember it like this

Formal = Boss decides: Command (same boss), Task (same job), Team.
Informal = Heart decides: Interest (same cause), Friendship (same vibe).

Exam line

Formal groups (command, task, team) are defined by the organization’s structure; informal groups (interest, friendship) arise naturally from social needs and common concerns.

5Why people join groups

There is no single reason. The slides give six:

🛡️

Security

Reduces the insecurity of “standing alone”.

First day in hostel, you quickly join a group so you aren’t alone.

🏅

Status

Being in an important group gives recognition.

“I’m in the GDG core team.”

💛

Self-esteem

Groups give feelings of self-worth.

Teammates value your ideas, you feel good about yourself.

👥

Affiliation

Fulfils social needs (belonging, friendship).

Friends to eat and talk with.

✊

Power

“Power in numbers”: what one can’t achieve alone, a group can.

One student complaining is ignored; 200 students signing a petition is not.

🎯

Goal achievement

Some tasks need many people’s talents, knowledge or power. Management uses formal groups for this.

A hackathon needs a coder, a designer and a presenter.

Remember it like this

“3 S + A P G” → Security, Status, Self-esteem, Affiliation, Power, Goal.

Story: “I joined the group to feel safe, look cool, feel good about myself, make friends, get strength in numbers, and get the job done.”

Why people form groups (second slide)

Need satisfaction

Social needs, security needs, esteem needs.

Proximity & attraction

Proximity = physical distance between employees (people sitting near each other become friends). Attraction comes from similar attitudes, performance or motivations.

The big model also adds group goals and economic reasons (a group can earn more, e.g. group bonus).

Example of proximity

Your closest friends in first year were probably your roommates or the people on your corridor. Nobody “planned” it; you were simply near each other.

6The five-stage model of group development

Every group grows up like a person: awkward, then fighting, then settling, then working, then saying goodbye. The names rhyme: Forming, Storming, Norming, Performing, Adjourning.

Stage 1 · Forming: uncertainty

Lots of uncertainty about the group’s purpose, structure and leadership. Members are “testing the waters” to see what behaviour is acceptable.

Stage 2 · Storming: intragroup conflict

  • Members accept the group exists, but resist the limits it puts on their individuality.
  • Conflict over who will control the group.
  • Interpersonal conflict as members become proactive and compete for roles.
  • Coalitions (small sub-groups) may form to influence goals and methods.
  • Members try to set norms and performance standards. This is a tenuous (weak) stage; the group can break here.

Stage 3 · Norming: close relationships & cohesiveness

  • Strong sense of group identity (“we”).
  • First real cohesion: roles are established, consensus forms around objectives.
  • Members develop common expectations about how goals should be achieved.

Stage 4 · Performing: fully functional

  • Structure is fully functional and accepted.
  • Energy moves from “getting to know each other” to doing the task.
  • Members coordinate and resolve conflicts efficiently.
  • Climate of mutual support: people feel safe taking risks, making mistakes, or asking for help.

Stage 5 · Adjourning: wrapping up

  • Only for temporary groups (task forces, project groups). For permanent work groups, performing is the last stage.
  • Attention goes to wrapping up activities, not performance.
  • Mixed feelings: some members are proud of what they achieved, others are sad about losing the friendships.
One example through all five stages: your OB group project
  1. Forming: WhatsApp group made. Everyone is polite, nobody knows who will lead or what the topic is.
  2. Storming: Two people want different companies as the topic. Arguments about who does the slides. A couple of friends team up against the others.
  3. Norming: Topic fixed, roles fixed (“you do research, I do slides, she presents”). People start saying “our project”.
  4. Performing: Work flows. Someone stuck on a slide just asks for help without ego.
  5. Adjourning: Presentation done. Some feel proud, some feel a bit sad the late-night calls are over.
Remember it like this

Hello → Fight → Rules → Work → Bye

Forming = Hello, Storming = Fight (storm = conflict), Norming = Rules (norms), Performing = Work, Adjourning = Bye (adjourn = end a meeting).

Exam line

Tuckman’s five stages: Forming (uncertainty), Storming (intragroup conflict), Norming (close relationships and cohesiveness), Performing (fully functional) and Adjourning (wrapping up, for temporary groups only).

7Group properties: Roles

Definition

Role: a set of expected behaviour patterns linked to someone holding a given position in a social unit.

In simple words

Your “role” is the character you play in a group. The same person plays many roles: son at home, student in class, captain on the ground, intern at office. Each role comes with a script of how you are “supposed” to behave.

TermMeaningExample
Role identityAttitudes and behaviours consistent with a role. When the role changes, the behaviour changes.A normal student becomes class representative and suddenly acts more serious and responsible. (Slide: a promotion brings new attitudes.)
Role perceptionMy own view of how I am supposed to act in a situation.A new intern thinks “interns should stay quiet in meetings” because that’s what they saw in movies.
Role expectationsHow others believe I should act.Slide example: people would be surprised to see an Imam selling concert tickets, because it doesn’t match what they expect of that role.
Psychological contractAn unwritten agreement about what management expects from the employee and vice versa: “work hard” in exchange for job security, promotion, raises and other rewards.Nobody writes “we’ll give you a raise if you stay late”, but both sides silently expect it.
Role conflictWhen a person faces divergent (clashing) role expectations.Your manager is also your close friend. As friend you want to cover for him; as employee you must report his mistake.

What if the psychological contract is broken?

Management breaks it

If management is derelict (negligent) in keeping its part, expect negative effects on employee performance and satisfaction.

Employees break it

If employees fail to meet expectations, the result is usually disciplinary action, up to and including firing.

Remember it like this

Identity = who I become · Perception = what I think I should do · Expectations = what others think I should do · Contract = the unwritten deal · Conflict = two scripts clashing.

8Group properties: Norms

Definition

Norms: acceptable standards of behaviour within a group that are shared by the group’s members.

In simple words

Norms are the unwritten rules of a group. Nobody prints them, but everyone knows them. Break one and you get the “look”.

Four common classes of norms

⚡

Performance norms

How hard to work, how to do the job, how much output, how late is “okay”. Extremely powerful in affecting performance.

Senior whispers: “Don’t finish too fast, you’ll make us look bad.”

👕

Appearance norms

Dress, loyalty to the group or organization, when to look busy. Some firms have formal dress codes.

Everyone in a bank wears formals; looking busy when the boss walks in.

🍱

Social arrangement norms

Come from informal groups; control social interaction: who eats lunch with whom, friendships on and off the job, games.

The interns always sit at the same lunch table.

💻

Allocation of resources norms

From the group or the organization: pay, who gets difficult jobs, who gets new tools.

The new laptop always goes to the senior first; the newest person gets the boring tasks.

Remember it like this

PASA: Performance, Appearance, Social arrangement, Allocation. Picture a new joiner asking 4 questions: “How hard do I work? What do I wear? Where do I sit for lunch? What do I get?”

Conformity

Conformity = adjusting your behaviour to match the group’s norms. When your opinion about objective facts is very different from everyone else’s, you feel strong pressure to change it to match theirs.

Example

Five friends confidently say the answer is B. You were sure it was C. You quietly change to B. Even when the facts are clear, the group pulls you.

Reference groups

Important groups that you belong to or hope to belong to, and whose norms you are likely to follow.

Example

You want to get into a top tech company, so you start copying how engineers there talk, dress and work, even before you join.

Deviant workplace behaviour

Antisocial actions by members that intentionally break established norms and harm the organization, its members, or both. Group norms can influence it: if your group does it, you are more likely to do it.

Example

Taking office supplies home, spreading rumours, taking 1-hour “tea breaks”. If the whole team takes long breaks, a new member soon starts too.

9Group properties: Status

Definition

Status: a socially defined position or rank given to groups or group members by others.

Effects of status on norms and conformity

  • High-status members get more freedom to break norms than others.
  • High-status people are better able to resist conformity pressure.
Example

The star salesperson walks into the meeting 15 minutes late and nobody says a word. If a new intern does that, it’s a problem. The college topper can disagree with the whole class and people still listen.

Remember it like this

Status = “rank in others’ eyes”. More status = more freedom from the rules.

10Group properties: Size & Social loafing

Definition

Social loafing: the tendency of individuals to put in less effort when working in a group than when working alone.

What causes social loafing?

  1. “Others aren’t doing their share”: if you see others as lazy or useless, you reduce your own effort to make it “fair” (re-establish equity).
  2. Dispersion of responsibility: the group result can’t be traced to any one person, so the link between my effort and the output is unclear. Hello, free-rider.
  3. In short: efficiency drops when people think their contribution cannot be measured.

Other conclusions on size

  • Odd-numbered groups do better than even (no deadlock in a vote).
  • Groups of 5 to 7 perform better overall than larger or smaller groups.
Example

A group project of 8 students where 2 do everything and 6 just add their names. Each of the 6 thinks, “Nobody will know what I did anyway.”

Remember it like this

“When everyone is responsible, no one is responsible.”  And the magic size: 5 to 7, odd is best (like a 5-judge bench).

Exam line

Social loafing is the tendency to exert less effort in a group than alone, caused by perceived inequity and dispersion of responsibility; it can be reduced by making individual contributions identifiable.

11Group properties: Cohesiveness

Definition

Cohesiveness: the degree to which members are attracted to each other and are motivated to stay in the group.

In simple words

Cohesiveness is the “glue”. A highly cohesive group feels like family; members don’t want to leave.

7 ways to increase cohesiveness

  1. Make the group smaller.
  2. Encourage agreement with group goals.
  3. Increase the time members spend together.
  4. Increase group status and the difficulty of getting in.
  5. Stimulate competition with other groups.
  6. Give rewards to the group, not individuals.
  7. Physically isolate the group.
Remember it like this: an army unit or an IIT hostel wing has all 7

Small unit ✔ · same mission ✔ · live together 24×7 ✔ · very hard to get in, proud name ✔ · rivalry with other units/hostels ✔ · the trophy goes to the whole wing ✔ · isolated campus ✔.

That’s why those groups are so tightly bonded. If you can picture the army unit, you can list all seven.

12Group decision making

  • Large groups are good at pooling information for complex tasks.
  • Smaller groups are better at coordinating and implementing complex tasks.
  • Simple, routine, standardized tasks don’t need very effective group processes for the group to do well.

Strengths

  • More complete information
  • Increased diversity of views
  • Higher quality (more accurate) decisions
  • Increased acceptance of solutions

Weaknesses

  • More time-consuming (slower)
  • Increased pressure to conform
  • Domination by one or a few members
  • Ambiguous (unclear) responsibility
Remember it like this

Strengths: “More brains → more info, more views, better answer, and everyone accepts it because they helped make it.”

Weaknesses = 4 S’s: Slow (time) · Sheep (conformity) · Shouters (domination) · Shrug (“not my responsibility”).

13Groupthink

Definition

Groupthink: a phenomenon in which the norm for consensus (everyone agreeing) overrides the realistic appraisal of alternative courses of action.

McShane: the tendency of highly cohesive groups to value consensus at the price of decision quality.

In simple words

The group wants harmony so badly that nobody questions the plan, even when it’s a bad plan. Unusual, minority or unpopular views are not seriously considered. The slides call it a “disease” that attacks many groups.

Slide example: NASA Challenger, 1986

Engineers raised concerns about the O-ring seals before the launch. They were criticized for it, because decision makers were under heavy pressure to launch after earlier delays and promises. The shuttle exploded. Key decision makers showed many groupthink symptoms.

Symptoms of groupthink

  1. Rationalize resistance: members explain away any evidence against their assumptions, and keep reinforcing them however strong the evidence is.
  2. Direct pressure on doubters: anyone who questions the majority view is pressured.
  3. Self-censorship: people with doubts stay silent and even convince themselves their doubts don’t matter.
  4. Illusion of unanimity: if someone doesn’t speak, it is assumed they fully agree.
Everyday example

Friends plan a late-night drive in heavy rain. One friend feels it’s unsafe but says nothing (self-censorship). Another who objects is called “boring” (pressure). Weather warnings are brushed off: “It’s just a little rain” (rationalizing). Everyone assumes all agree (illusion of unanimity).

Remember it like this

Groupthink is “Yes-men disease.” Its 4 symptoms in order: Excuse it → Pressure them → Shut up → Assume yes.

Exam line

Groupthink occurs when pressure for conformity and consensus prevents a cohesive group from critically evaluating alternatives, e.g. NASA’s Challenger disaster (1986).

14Groupshift

Definition

Groupshift: a change in decision risk between the group’s decision and the individual decision a member would make alone. It can be toward more caution or more risk.

  • Groupshift is a special case of groupthink.
  • Discussion makes members move to a more extreme position in the direction they already leaned. Cautious people become more cautious; aggressive people take more risk. Discussion exaggerates the initial position.
  • Sometimes the group is more conservative, but more often the shift is toward greater risk.
  • Why toward risk? The group diffuses responsibility. If the decision fails, no single member can be fully blamed, so the group dares more.
Example

Alone, each of 4 friends would invest ₹5,000 in a risky stock. After hyping each other up in a group call, they put in ₹40,000 together. “If it fails, it’s all of us, not just me.”

Remember it like this

Group discussion is a volume knob: whatever the group already felt, it turns it up. And it usually turns up risk, because blame is shared.

Groupthink

Everyone agrees, nobody questions. Problem = poor quality of decision.

Groupshift

Group goes more extreme than members alone. Problem = risk level changes.

15Group decision-making techniques

🗣️

Interacting groups

The usual meeting: members talk face-to-face, using verbal and nonverbal communication.

Problem: members censor themselves and pressure each other to conform.

💡

Brainstorming

An idea-generation process that encourages any and all alternatives while withholding criticism.

📝

Nominal Group Technique (NGT)

Members meet face-to-face but think and judge independently in a systematic way.

💻

Electronic meeting

Members interact on computers: comments are anonymous and votes are aggregated.

Rules of brainstorming

  • Do not criticize ideas.
  • Give as many ideas as possible.
  • Say every idea that comes to mind, no matter how wild.
  • Combine and improve others’ ideas.

Nominal Group Technique: 4 steps

  1. Write silently: before any discussion, each member independently writes down ideas on the problem.
  2. Present one by one: each member presents one idea at a time, in turns, until all ideas are recorded. No discussion until everything is recorded.
  3. Discuss: the group discusses the ideas for clarity and evaluates them.
  4. Rank silently: each member independently rank-orders the ideas. The idea with the highest total ranking wins.
Remember it like this

“Nominal” means “in name only”: it is a group only in name, because people think alone. Steps: Write → Read out → Discuss → Rank. Silent at the start, silent at the end.

Why use it? It stops the loud person from dominating and reduces conformity pressure.

16Group norms & the Hawthorne studies

A series of studies by Elton Mayo at Western Electric Company’s Hawthorne Works, Chicago, between 1924 and 1932.

Conclusions

  1. Worker behaviour and sentiments (feelings) were closely related.
  2. Group influences (norms) significantly affected individual behaviour.
  3. Group standards were highly effective in setting individual worker output.
  4. Money was less important in deciding output than group standards, sentiments and security.
In simple words

The researchers expected that things like money and working conditions would decide how much people produce. Instead they found that the group decides. If your group’s “normal” output is 100 pieces a day, you will make about 100, even if extra money is offered for more.

Remember it like this

Hawthorne = “Hey, the group sets the speed, not the salary.” Mayo · Chicago · 1924 to 1932.

Exam line

The Hawthorne studies (Elton Mayo, 1924 to 1932) showed that group norms and social factors influence worker output more than money.

17Quick revision & self-test

The whole PDF in 12 lines

  • Synergy: whole > sum of parts. Team has positive synergy; group just shares info.
  • Formal: command, task, team. Informal: interest, friendship.
  • Why join: Security, Status, Self-esteem, Affiliation, Power, Goal (3S + APG).
  • Stages: Forming (uncertainty), Storming (conflict), Norming (cohesion), Performing (functional), Adjourning (wrap-up).
  • Roles: identity, perception (mine), expectations (others’), psychological contract (unwritten deal), conflict (clash).
  • Norms (PASA): Performance, Appearance, Social arrangement, Allocation of resources.
  • Conformity = adjusting to the group; reference group = group you belong to or want to.
  • Deviant behaviour = intentional antisocial norm-breaking; group norms influence it.
  • Status: rank given by others; high status = freedom to deviate.
  • Social loafing: less effort in groups; best size 5 to 7, odd numbers.
  • Groupthink: consensus over quality (Challenger). Groupshift: group goes more extreme, usually riskier.
  • NGT: write, present, discuss, rank. Hawthorne: Mayo, norms beat money.
