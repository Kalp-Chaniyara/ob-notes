1What is perception?

Definition (standard textbook)

Perception is the process by which we organize and interpret what our senses pick up, to give meaning to our surroundings.

In simple words

Your eyes only collect light. Your brain decides what it means. Two people can look at the same thing and “see” different things. That is perception.

The PDF opens with optical illusions to prove this:

🐘

“How many legs does this elephant have?”

The legs and the gaps between them are drawn so cleverly that you can’t count them. Your brain keeps switching.

👵

Old woman or young girl?

One picture, two people. Hint from slide: the old woman’s nose is the young girl’s chin.

🌀

Other illusions

A spinning circle that isn’t moving, a triangle that isn’t drawn, dots that appear in a grid, faces hidden in paintings and clouds.

Why this matters at work

A manager sees an employee leaving at 6 pm sharp as “lazy”; another sees “efficient, finishes on time”. Same fact, different perception. Decisions about people depend on perception, not just facts.

The slides then show the Gestalt principles (German “Gestalt” = whole form): rules our brain uses to organize what it sees into meaningful wholes.

Remember the 4 Gestalt laws in the PDF

F-P-S-C: Figure-ground, Proximity, Similarity, Closure. Think “Friends Play Same Cricket”.

2Figure-ground (and the Law of Prägnanz)

In simple words

Your brain splits any scene into a figure (the main thing you focus on) and the ground (background). Some images are designed so both can be the figure, so the picture “flips”.

The slides show famous logos built on this:

  • visitnorway.com logo (slide title: Law of Prägnanz, figure-ground)
  • GNOME desktop logo: a foot whose shape also forms the letter G
  • Macintosh (Mac) logo: a front face and a side profile share the same outline
Law of Prägnanz

Also called the law of simplicity or “good figure”: we see things in the simplest, most stable form possible.

Exam line

Figure-ground: we perceive an object (figure) as standing out from its background (ground); in reversible images such as Rubin’s vase, figure and ground can swap.

3Law of proximity

Definition

Objects that are close to each other are seen as forming a group.

Slide example: MTV Europe Music Awards 2002

The MTV logo and the Europe Music Awards logo sit together in the top left; the sponsors’ logos (D&G, Vodafone, Foot Locker, American Express) sit together in the bottom right. White space between them tells you “organisers” vs “sponsors” without a single word.

Remember it like this

Proximity = “Near means together.” People sitting at the same table in a café look like one group.

4Law of similarity

Definition

Elements that are similar to each other (shape, colour, size) are grouped together and seen as a unit.

Remember it like this

Similarity = “Same looks, same group.” In a stadium you instantly see fans of one team as one block because they wear the same jersey colour.

5Law of closure

Definition

We mentally close up or complete objects that are not actually complete.

Slide examples
  • IBM logo: it’s just horizontal white/blue stripes of different lengths, but you read “I B M”.
  • 8 small circles in a ring: we perceive one big circle, not 8 separate circles.
Remember it like this

Closure = “Brain fills the gaps.” Like reading “I l_ve y_u” perfectly even with missing letters.

6What is motivation?

Definitions (slide)

Motivation comes from the word “motive”, which means needs, desires, wants or drives within a person. It is the process of stimulating people to act to accomplish goals.

Motivation is the process that initiates, guides and maintains goal-oriented behaviour. It involves biological, emotional, social and cognitive forces.

Motivation is the desire to achieve a goal or a certain performance level, leading to goal-directed behaviour.

In simple words

Motivation answers “WHY does a person do something?” It is the inner push that starts you, steers you and keeps you going.

Remember it like this

Motivation is a car journey: it starts the engine (initiates), steers (guides), and keeps fuel in the tank (maintains). Fuel types: Body, Heart, People, Mind = biological, emotional, social, cognitive.

The slides add: motivation explains why we set goals, chase achievement and power, want closeness with others, and feel emotions like fear, anger and compassion. It helps us understand where motivation comes from, why it changes, and what raises or lowers it. It leads to valued outcomes like better performance, well-being, personal growth and a sense of purpose.

7Performance = Motivation × Ability × Environment

  • Motivation: trying hard. Important, but not sufficient on its own.
  • Ability: having the skills and knowledge for the job. Sometimes the key factor.
  • Environment: having the resources, information and support needed.
Slide example

For someone sweeping the floor, motivation matters most. But even the most motivated person cannot design a house without the talent (ability) for it.

Remember it like this

It’s multiplication, not addition: if any one is zero, performance is zero. Super-motivated + no skill = nothing. Skilled + motivated + no laptop = nothing.

8Extrinsic vs Intrinsic motivation

Extrinsic (from outside)

Something external pushes you. Easiest example: money. You go to work for the paycheck, not because you love it. Can also be intangible, like praise from someone you respect.

Intrinsic (from inside)

Comes from within. Running a marathon to feel proud of yourself, not for the medal. Harder to create, because people are moved by very different things and it’s hard to get inside someone’s head.

Remember it like this

EXtrinsic = EXternal (reward from outside). INtrinsic = INside (joy from inside). Studying for marks = extrinsic; studying because the subject fascinates you = intrinsic.

Ways to motivate employees with extrinsic motivation

Merit-based raises

A bigger paycheck that feels earned keeps people working hard and shows hard work is rewarded.

Bonuses

As a standing reward for hitting a target, or a surprise for a job well done.

Promote from within

Many people link success with climbing the corporate ladder and push themselves for bigger positions.

Team-based goal rewards

A goal set by someone else, with a reward, builds collaboration.

The slides also list: individual goal rewards, parties, awards, competitions with prizes, regular evaluations, breaking work into milestones and sub-goals.

And these (more on the intrinsic side): more autonomy, more flexibility, chances for personal growth, helping employees set their own goals, diverse sources of inspiration, a positive environment, making the job more interesting, time for self-reflection.

9Map of motivation theories

1. Need-based theories

Answer: WHAT do people need?

  • Maslow’s hierarchy
  • Herzberg’s two-factor
  • McClelland’s acquired needs

2. Process-based theories

Answer: HOW do people decide to put in effort?

  • Equity theory
  • Expectancy theory
  • Reinforcement theory
Remember it like this

Need theories are about the menu (what you’re hungry for). Process theories are about the decision (is it worth ordering? is it fair?).

Need = “MHM” (Maslow, Herzberg, McClelland). Process = “EER” (Equity, Expectancy, Reinforcement).

10Maslow’s hierarchy of needs

LevelWhat it meansAt the workplace
1. PhysiologicalBasic survival: food, water, sleep, shelter, warmthA place to work, regular monthly salary, comfortable environment, basic facilities (tea/coffee), cafeteria
2. SafetySecurity, stability, order, freedom from fearFormal employment contract, pension, sick pay, job security, health & safety, no violence at work
3. Social (belonging, love)Friends, family, acceptance, affectionTeam work across departments, team-building activities, good relations with co-workers and managers; for remote work: Teams/Zoom, online quizzes, virtual get-togethers
4. EsteemRespect from others + self-respect, being seen as useful and importantPraise, job title, status, more responsibility, 360-degree feedback, peer recognition programmes, awards, promotions
5. Self-actualizationBecoming the best you can be; reaching full potentialPersonal development plans, training, mentoring, challenging assignments, creative work, fast-track management programmes, career discussions

Key features

  • Needs are arranged in a hierarchy from lower to higher.
  • Lower needs generally must be satisfied before higher ones become dominant.
  • A satisfied need no longer strongly motivates.
  • Different people may prioritize needs differently.
Example

A hungry daily-wage worker cares about getting enough money for food (physiological), not about a fancy job title. A well-paid senior engineer with a secure job now wants recognition and challenging projects (esteem, self-actualization). Slide example of self-actualization: a professor doing innovative research, or an artist creating original work.

Spiritual interpretation & Self-transcendence

  • Physiological: the body is cared for as the instrument through which life is experienced.
  • Safety: inner faith, trust and freedom from fear.
  • Love & belonging: compassion, love, connection with others.
  • Esteem: self-worth from inner dignity and character, less from outside praise.
  • Self-actualization: becoming fully what one can be; creativity, authenticity, peak experiences.
Self-transcendence (Maslow’s later idea, above self-actualization)

Going beyond personal desires, ego and achievement to connect with something larger than oneself: humanity, nature, truth, or God. It means serving others, experiencing unity or devotion, and seeking truth, beauty and meaning rather than personal achievement.

Remember it like this (Hinglish ladder)

Pet → Suraksha → Saathi → Sammaan → Sapna → Seva

Pet (stomach = physiological) → Suraksha (safety) → Saathi (friends = social) → Sammaan (respect = esteem) → Sapna (dream = self-actualization) → Seva (serving others = self-transcendence).

Exam line

Maslow proposed five needs in a hierarchy: physiological, safety, social, esteem and self-actualization; lower needs must be largely satisfied before higher needs motivate, and a satisfied need no longer motivates.

11Herzberg’s Two-Factor theory

Frederick Herzberg said the things that make you unhappy at work are different from the things that make you motivated.

Hygiene factors

Company policy, supervision & relationships, working conditions, salary, safety & security.

Missing → dissatisfied. Present → you just take them for granted (not motivated).

These are around the job (context).

Motivators

Achievement, recognition, interesting work, increased responsibility, advancement & growth.

Present → truly motivated to try harder.

These are intrinsic to the job (content).

Slide example

Your office is too hot in summer and too cold in winter, and you are harassed. You are miserable. Now fix it: perfect temperature, no harassment. Are you motivated? Not really; you just take it for granted. These are hygiene factors: we miss them when absent but don’t notice them when present.

Remember it like this

Hygiene = brushing your teeth. Brushing doesn’t make you happy, but not brushing causes problems. Hygiene = “Hurts if missing.”

Motivators = “Make you move.” They’re about the work itself: achievement, recognition, growth.

It is not that simple (slide nuance)

  • Pay is classed as hygiene, but it can have symbolic value: a raise can show you are recognized and advancing.
  • The quality of supervision (hygiene) can decide whether you get interesting work, recognition and more responsibility (motivators).

Herzberg’s 6 motivations (slide list)

  1. Recognition and status from managers and management
  2. Growth and promotional opportunities
  3. Meaningful and challenging work
  4. Reward of achievement from the job
  5. Own responsibility for work
  6. Advancement opportunities
Careful: one slide has the labels swapped

The slide titled “Herzberg’s Theory of Motivation” (with the green ➕ and red ➖) lists salary, working conditions, regular 1-2-1s, feedback, development discussions under “Motivator Factors” and achievement, recognition, work itself, extra responsibility, giving a presentation under “Hygiene Factors”. That is backwards compared to Herzberg and to the very next slide. Learn it the way shown above. If unsure what your professor wants for that specific slide, check with them.

Exam line

Herzberg: hygiene factors (pay, policy, supervision, conditions, security) prevent dissatisfaction but don’t motivate; motivators (achievement, recognition, work itself, responsibility, growth) create real motivation.

12McClelland’s Acquired-Needs theory

David McClelland: we acquire (learn) three needs through life experience, and one usually dominates.

🏆

Need for Achievement (nAch)

Strong need to succeed and keep improving performance.

“The Topper”

👑

Need for Power (nPow)

Want to influence others and control the environment.

“The Leader”

🤗

Need for Affiliation (nAff)

Want approval of peers and supervisors, good relationships.

“The Friend”

More about high achievers (the slides focus on them)

  • As children they were often praised for hard work, which built persistence. (Praise for intelligence can actually hurt children’s motivation and performance.)
  • Suited to jobs with clear goals and immediate feedback, like sales. Also do well as entrepreneurs, scientists and engineers.
  • Prefer merit-based organizations that reward performance, not seniority.

Are high achievers good managers? Often not.

  • They get promoted because they shine as individuals. But management is about getting work done through others.
  • They may see coaching, communicating and meetings as a waste of time.
  • They enjoy doing things themselves, so they struggle to delegate, micromanage, and can become overbearing bosses expecting everyone to be as dedicated.
Example

The best salesperson is promoted to sales manager. Now the job is recruiting, training and motivating others, not selling. She keeps jumping in to close deals herself and nitpicks her team. Great achiever, weak manager.

What managers should do

Find each employee’s dominant need: achievers respond to goals, power people seek influence, affiliation people seek approval. And warn high achievers about the management pitfalls above.

Remember it like this

“APA”: Achievement, Power, Affiliation = Topper, Leader, Friend.

13Equity theory

Slide story

You earn $10/hour as an office assistant. 6 months in, you’re good, creative, helpful, stay late, flexible. A new person is hired, same title, same tasks, a bit better at computers (not clearly needed), at $14/hour. How do you feel? “This is unfair!” Would you still go above and beyond? Equity theory explains that feeling.

Inputs

What you feel you contribute: hard work, loyalty, time with the organization, education, training, skills.

Outcomes

The rewards you perceive: pay (core), acknowledgement or preferential treatment from a manager (peripheral).

The person reasons: “I’ve been here 6 months, loyal, perform well (my inputs), I get $10 (my outcome). The new person has no experience here (referent’s inputs) but gets $14. Unfair.”

Remember it like this

The sibling chocolate rule. You’re fine with one chocolate until your sibling gets two for doing the same chores. It’s not about the amount, it’s about the comparison.

Exam line

Equity theory: people compare their own ratio of outcomes to inputs with that of a referent; perceived unfairness reduces motivation.

14Expectancy theory (Victor Vroom)

Motivation comes from a rational calculation: people weigh up their situation before deciding how much effort to put in.

PartQuestion in your headClass example (slide)
ExpectancyIf I try hard, will I perform well?Do you believe effort in a class leads to doing well in it?
InstrumentalityIf I perform well, will I get rewarded?Does a good grade lead to a better job, or approval from instructor, friends, parents?
ValenceDo I actually want that reward?Do you value the better job or that approval?
Remember it like this

Three questions: “Can I? → Will I get? → Do I want?” It’s multiplication: if any answer is “no” (zero), motivation is zero.

How managers can influence each part

Expectancy

Make sure employees have the right skills and knowledge; make the environment support performance; encourage people so they believe effort makes a difference.

Instrumentality

Link pay and rewards to performance (bonuses, awards, merit pay); tell people about rewards in advance (publicize contests); remove non-performance influences on rewards.

Valence

Find rewards employees actually value (talk to them, survey them); make sure rewards are seen as fair; give a choice between rewards.

Slide warning: the rotating “employee of the month”

If the award just rotates among everyone, people stop believing performance is rewarded. This meritless reward can actually reduce the motivation of top performers by eroding instrumentality.

15Reinforcement theory

Based on Ivan Pavlov’s behavioural conditioning and B. F. Skinner’s operant conditioning (Science and Human Behavior, 1953). Simple idea: behaviour followed by good consequences is repeated; behaviour followed by bad consequences is reduced. We learn like this from infancy; watch a small child exploring.

Goal: INCREASE good behaviourGoal: DECREASE bad behaviour
ADD somethingPositive reinforcement
Add a pleasant thing
Manager praises the employee		Punishment
Add an unpleasant thing
Warning for being late; demotion
REMOVE somethingNegative reinforcement
Remove an unpleasant thing
Manager stops nagging once report is done		Extinction
Remove the reward
Manager ignores the behaviour

➕ Positive reinforcement

Positive behaviour → positive consequence. Praise an employee for treating a customer respectfully. If praise comes immediately, they link behaviour and reward and repeat it.

➖ Negative reinforcement

Positive behaviour → unpleasant thing removed. Nagging continues until the report is done. Kids do chores to stop the nagging. Studying to avoid poor grades. Risk: the person may just avoid the manager instead.

🔇 Extinction

Negative behaviour → reward taken away. A coworker forwards joke emails because people laugh and reply. Ignore them and they stop. (People rewarded for unethical behaviour do more of it.)

⚠️ Punishment

Negative behaviour → negative consequence. A warning for being consistently late to work.

Most common confusion

Negative reinforcement is NOT punishment. Any “reinforcement” increases behaviour. “Negative” only means something is taken away (the nagging). Punishment decreases behaviour.

Remember it like this

Positive/Negative = add/remove (maths sign, not good/bad). Reinforcement = more of the behaviour. Punishment/Extinction = less. Extinction = the behaviour “dies out” like an extinct dinosaur because nobody feeds it attention.

16What is learning?

Definition

Learning: any relatively permanent change in behaviour that occurs as a result of experience.

So learning:

  1. Involves change
  2. Is relatively permanent
  3. Is acquired through experience
Example

You touched a hot pan once, and now you always use a cloth. That’s learning: a change, it lasts, and it came from experience. Being tired and slow one evening is not learning (temporary).

Remember it like this

C-P-E: Change, Permanent, Experience. “Learning leaves a mark.”

Four theories / ideas in the slides: classical conditioning, operant conditioning, social learning, shaping.

17Classical conditioning (Pavlov)

Definition (slide)

A type of conditioning in which a person responds to a stimulus that would not ordinarily produce such a response.

First, the English words

WordSimple meaning
StimulusA trigger: something that happens and makes you react (a smell, a sound, a sight)
ResponseYour reaction to that trigger
UnconditionedNatural, not learned. You are born with it; nobody taught you
ConditionedLearned through training or repetition
SalivationYour mouth watering
Meat pasteJust meat food given to the dog

So “unconditioned stimulus” = natural trigger, and “conditioned response” = learned reaction.

Pavlov’s dog, step by step

  1. Before training: give the dog food and its mouth waters. Nobody taught it this; it’s natural. Food = natural trigger, mouth watering = natural reaction.
  2. Bell alone: ring a bell and nothing happens. A bell means nothing to a dog.
  3. Training: every time food is given, ring the bell at the same moment. Bell + food, bell + food, again and again for many days.
  4. After training: ring only the bell, with no food, and the dog’s mouth still waters. The dog has learned “bell = food is coming”.

Unconditioned stimulusNatural triggerFood (meat paste)Dinner aroma (smell)
Unconditioned responseNatural reactionMouth waters at foodMouth waters at the smell

Conditioned responseLearned reactionMouth waters at the bell aloneMouth waters when the news starts
The slide’s 2nd example: dinner aroma & 6 o’clock news

A family eats dinner every evening at exactly 6 o’clock, which is also when the news starts on TV. The smell of dinner makes your mouth water (natural). The news is just a TV programme, nothing to do with food. But for months, the news always starts at the same time as the food smell arrives. One day you’re not even having dinner, the news music starts, and your mouth waters anyway.

Smell of dinner = Pavlov’s food. 6 o’clock news = Pavlov’s bell.

Example you already know

In school, the lunch bell rang every day just before you opened your tiffin. At first it was just a noise. After a few months, the moment it rang you felt hungry, even before seeing any food. The bell became a learned trigger for hunger.

Remember it like this

Put something natural together with something neutral, many times. Eventually the neutral thing alone gives the same reaction.

Un-conditioned = un-learned (natural). Conditioned = learned. Classical conditioning is passive: it happens TO you.

Exam line

Classical conditioning is learning in which a neutral stimulus (bell), after being repeatedly paired with a natural stimulus (food), starts producing the same response (salivation) on its own.

18Operant conditioning (Skinner)

Definition

A type of conditioning in which desired voluntary behaviour leads to a reward or prevents a punishment.

Key concepts: reflexive (unlearned) behaviour, conditioned (learned) behaviour, reinforcement.

Example

A salesperson gets a commission for every sale, so they choose to make more calls. A student submits on time to avoid a late penalty.

Classical

Involuntary, reflex. Stimulus comes before the response. Passive (bell → salivate).

Operant

Voluntary, chosen. Consequence comes after the behaviour. Active (work → bonus).

Remember it like this

Operant = you OPERATE on the world (you choose to act) to get a reward. Classical = it happens to you.

19Social learning theory (Albert Bandura)

Definition

People can learn through observation and direct experience: by watching, imitating and modeling others.

Slide example

A teacher who is positive and encouraging with students builds their self-efficacy: the belief in one’s own ability to succeed in different situations.

Modeling (slide)

A model is a person or thing considered an excellent example of something (Oxford, 2012). Modeling is learning new information, skills or behaviour through observation.

4 processes of social learning

  1. Attentional: you must notice the model. We pay attention to attractive, important, similar models.
  2. Retention: you must remember what you saw.
  3. Motor reproduction: you must be able to do it yourself (practice).
  4. Reinforcement (motivation): you must want to do it, usually because it gets rewarded.
Remember it like this

Learning cricket by watching Virat Kohli: you watch (Attention), remember his cover drive (Retention), practice it (Reproduction), and keep doing it because friends cheer (Reinforcement). “Watch, Remember, Copy, Cheer.”

20Shaping behaviour & types of reinforcement

Definition

Shaping: systematically reinforcing each successive step that moves a person closer to the desired response.

Example

A new intern is always 30 minutes late. Instead of waiting for perfect punctuality, the manager praises them when they’re 20 min late, then 10, then on time. Small steps, each rewarded.

Key concepts

  • Reinforcement is required to change behaviour.
  • Some rewards work better than others.
  • The timing of reinforcement affects how fast and how permanently we learn.

Types of reinforcement (slide definitions)

TypeDefinition
Positive reinforcementProviding a reward for a desired behaviour.
Negative reinforcementRemoving an unpleasant consequence when the desired behaviour occurs.
PunishmentApplying an undesirable condition to eliminate an undesirable behaviour.
ExtinctionWithholding reinforcement of a behaviour to cause it to stop.
Remember it like this

Shaping is like teaching a puppy to sit: treat for bending legs, treat for half sitting, treat for full sit.

21Quick revision & self-test

The whole PDF in 14 lines

  • Perception: organizing and interpreting what we sense; same thing, different views.
  • Gestalt laws: Figure-ground (Rubin vase, Mac/GNOME/Norway logos), Proximity (MTV awards), Similarity (circles & triangle rows), Closure (IBM logo, 8 circles).
  • Motivation: from “motive”; initiates, guides, maintains goal-directed behaviour.
  • Performance = Motivation × Ability × Environment.
  • Extrinsic = external rewards (money, praise); intrinsic = from inside.
  • Need theories: Maslow, Herzberg, McClelland. Process theories: Equity, Expectancy, Reinforcement.
  • Maslow: physiological → safety → social → esteem → self-actualization (+ self-transcendence).
  • Herzberg: hygiene (pay, policy, conditions) prevent dissatisfaction; motivators (achievement, recognition, growth) motivate.
  • McClelland: achievement, power, affiliation; high achievers often weak managers.
  • Equity: compare my outcome/input with others’ ($10 vs $14).
  • Expectancy (Vroom): Expectancy × Instrumentality × Valence.
  • Reinforcement: positive & negative increase behaviour; punishment & extinction decrease it.
  • Learning: relatively permanent change from experience. Classical (Pavlov, bell), Operant (voluntary → reward).
  • Social learning (Bandura): attention, retention, motor reproduction, reinforcement. Shaping: reward each step closer.

Test yourself (tap to reveal)

The IBM logo is made of stripes but we read “IBM”. Which law?
Law of closure: we complete incomplete shapes.
In the MTV awards poster, why do we see organisers and sponsors as two groups?
Law of proximity: objects close together are seen as a group; white space separates them.
Why is performance a product (×) and not a sum?
Because if motivation, ability or environment is zero, performance is zero no matter how high the others are.
Is salary a hygiene factor or a motivator in Herzberg?
Hygiene factor, though it can have symbolic value as recognition.
Why may high achievers make poor managers?
They see coaching and meetings as a waste of time, struggle to delegate, micromanage and expect everyone to be as dedicated.
What are expectancy, instrumentality and valence?
Expectancy: effort → performance. Instrumentality: performance → reward. Valence: how much the reward is valued.
How can a rotating employee-of-the-month award hurt motivation?
It is not tied to performance, so it erodes instrumentality, especially for top performers.
A manager stops nagging once the report is submitted. Which type?
Negative reinforcement (removing something unpleasant to increase desired behaviour).
Ignoring a coworker’s joke emails so they stop sending them is…?
Extinction: withholding reinforcement so the behaviour stops.
In the 6 o’clock news example, what is the conditioned stimulus?
The 6 o’clock news. Dinner aroma is the unconditioned stimulus; salivation is the response.
Name the four processes of social learning.
Attentional, retention, motor reproduction, reinforcement.
